# Writing Style Guide — Challenge Card Copy

Use this file whenever you write or rewrite copy for challenge cards, drops, or
any short-form description on this site (titles, blurbs, descriptions on
"Recent Drops" and similar). Read it in full before writing anything, then
run the checklist at the bottom against your draft before shipping it.

This combines two things: rules specific to how *this app's* copy should
sound (Part 1), and a general reference of AI-writing tells adapted from the
[avoid-ai-writing](https://github.com/conorbronsdon/avoid-ai-writing) skill,
trimmed to what actually applies to short punchy card copy (Part 2).

If you only have time to internalize one thing: **describe the mechanics,
then get out of the way.** Don't tell the reader the activity is funny,
hilarious, or comedy gold — describe what physically happens and let them
decide.

---

## Part 1 — This app's voice

**Register:** a friend describing a game to you over text, not a copywriter
selling it. Second person where natural, present tense, active voice.

**Sentence rhythm:** short instruction, short instruction, one longer
sentence if needed. Don't build every description as setup → escalation →
payoff-with-adjective-pileup. Vary length card to card — not every card needs
the same shape.

**Payoff:** let the mechanic imply the fun. If you catch yourself writing a
sentence whose only job is to tell the reader an outcome is funny, delete it
or replace it with what actually happens.

**Fun is not optional.** Cutting the hype words is the first half of the job,
not the whole job. What's left has to still sound like someone who wants you
to play this: contractions, an aside, an opinion, a warning about your floor.
A card that reads like assembly instructions has failed in the other
direction. If a draft is technically clean and completely flat, it isn't done.

**It has to make literal sense.** Every card is a set of instructions someone
who has never seen the challenge should be able to follow. Name a thing
before you refer to it — you can't write "holds the loop low" when no loop
has been introduced. Don't cut a clause if the sentence stops parsing without
it. Read the draft as a person standing in their living room with a phone:
can they actually do this?

**Don't over-correct into staccato.** The rhythm rule asks for variety, not
for five clipped commands in a row. Fragments are seasoning. If most of the
card is four-word sentences, it reads like a robot that was told to sound
casual, which is its own tell.

### Patterns specific to this content — always cut

These showed up repeatedly in the current cards and are the fastest tell
that a description was machine-written. Treat all of them as hard no's, not
judgment calls.

**Dash-attached similes.** A sentence action followed by an em dash and a
comparison: `— like threading through a human hoop`. If the comparison is
worth keeping, either open with it or give it its own sentence. Usually it's
not worth keeping — cut it.

**Adjective-stacked rule-of-three closers.** Nearly every card in the sample
ends a paragraph with three reaction-nouns in a row: `hilarious slow-motion
fails, surprise tumbles, and uncontrollable laughter`. Pick one concrete
outcome, or cut the closer entirely and end on the last instruction.

**"Equal parts X and Y" / "X parts Y and Z" formula.** `It's equal parts
acting exercise and comedy gold.` This is a stock summarizing sentence that
could be pasted onto any card unchanged. Delete it — if the card needs a
closer, make it a specific detail (a prop, a phrase, a number), not a genre
label.

**Pre-labeling the fun.** Words that tell the reader how to feel instead of
showing them: `hilarious`, `comedy gold`, `sparks a hilarious argument`,
`reveals who really knows the relationship best`. If the mechanic is
actually funny, the description of what happens will carry that on its own.
Cut the adjective and check whether the sentence still works — it almost
always does, and it's stronger without it.

**"The goal is to..." throat-clearing.** State the action directly instead
of framing it as a stated objective. `The goal is to nail each delivery
without cracking up` → `Try to get through it without cracking up.`

**Vague promise of insight.** `reveals who really knows the relationship
best` describes an emotional payoff no card can actually guarantee. Either
name the concrete mechanism that produces it (a running score, a tally,
something on screen) or cut the claim.

### Worked example

**Before (current site copy):**

> One person says a set phrase (like 'are you serious?') and their partner
> or friend has to repeat it back in four completely different emotional
> tones — flirty, disgusted, shocked, and deadpan — all back-to-back as fast
> as possible. The goal is to nail each delivery without cracking up, while
> the other person films the whole sequence and drops in their live reaction
> to each version. It's equal parts acting exercise and comedy gold.

**After:**

> One person picks a phrase — "are you serious?" works well — and says it.
> Their partner repeats it back four times in a row: flirty, disgusted,
> shocked, deadpan. No breaks, no do-overs. Whoever's filming reacts live to
> each version and cuts the reactions into the clip. Most runs end before
> take four.

What changed: cut the dash simile and the "equal parts" closer, replaced
"the goal is to nail each delivery without cracking up" with a direct
instruction plus a concrete, checkable detail ("most runs end before take
four") instead of a genre label.

### Second worked example — the other failure mode

A draft can pass every rule above and still be bad. This one did:

> One of you clasps both hands together and holds the loop low. The other has
> to get their whole body through it, one leg first, then ducking their head
> under, without either of you breaking grip. Sounds simple. Almost nobody
> clears it on the first try.

Two problems. "The loop" shows up out of nowhere, so the very first
instruction can't be followed by anyone who hasn't already seen the
challenge. And the voice is gone: it reads like a manual, with no opinion and
nothing that sounds like a person who has watched this go wrong.

**Fixed:**

> One of you clasps your hands together and holds them low, so your arms make
> a hoop in front of you. Your partner's job is to get their entire body
> through that hoop, one leg at a time, ducking their head under without
> either of you breaking the grip. It looks like a two-second trick. It is
> not. Whoever's holding the hoop usually ends up on the floor with them.

The hoop is built before it's used, and the last line has a point of view
without ever saying the word "funny".

## Part 2 — General AI-writing tells (reference)

These apply to any prose you write for this project, not just cards. Trimmed
from the full avoid-ai-writing skill to the parts relevant to short
marketing/product copy — the source skill also covers blog posts, investor
emails, and social posts, which don't apply here.

### Formatting

- **Em dashes:** target zero in card copy. If you reach for one, rewrite as
  two sentences or a comma.
- **Bold overuse:** don't bold phrases for emphasis in body copy; if
  something needs emphasis, lead the sentence with it instead.
- **Bullet lists:** card descriptions are prose, not bullets. Save bullets
  for genuinely list-like content (a numbered rule sequence, for example).

### Sentence-level tells

- **"It's not X — it's Y" / split-negation:** rewrite as a direct positive
  statement.
- **Hollow intensifiers:** cut `genuine`, `genuinely`, `real` (as
  intensifier), `truly`. State the fact plainly.
- **Vague endorsement:** don't write "worth trying" — say what specifically
  makes it worth trying.
- **Hedging:** cut `perhaps`, `could potentially`, `it's important to note
  that`.
- **Rule of three:** vary groupings. Two, four, or a full sentence instead
  of a triad — see Part 1, this is the single biggest tell in the current
  copy.

### Vocabulary to replace

Words that skew heavily toward machine-generated text. Individually fine in
small doses, but two or more in one card is a signal to rewrite.

| Replace | With |
|---|---|
| delve / delve into | explore, dig into, look at |
| unleash | let loose, release, start |
| elevate | improve, raise, level up |
| seamless / seamlessly | smooth, easy |
| game-changer / game-changing | (describe what specifically changes) |
| next level | (say what's different, or cut) |
| epic / legendary (as filler) | (cut, or name the specific thing that earns it) |
| unforgettable | (describe the specific moment, or cut) |
| ultimate | (name what makes it the best version, or cut) |
| perfect for | good for, made for |
| dive into / deep dive | try, start, get into |
| showcase | show |
| journey (metaphor) | (name the actual activity) |
| moment (vague, "a moment of...") | (name the specific thing that happens) |

Flag these when two or more appear in the same card — individually harmless,
but clustering is the tell:

`harness`, `unlock`, `spark` (as verb — "sparks laughter," "sparks
connection"), `fuel`, `ignite`, `elevate`, `curated`, `crafted`,
`experience` (as a noun standing in for "activity" or "game").

### Structural and rhythm signals

- **Uniform sentence length:** if every sentence in a card is 15–20 words,
  it reads robotic. Mix a short instruction with a longer one.
- **Formulaic shape:** don't let every card follow the same
  setup → escalation → adjective-pileup-payoff structure. If you read five
  cards in a row and they all end the same way, rewrite.
- **Read-aloud test:** read the card out loud. If it sounds like it could be
  read by a text-to-speech voice without sounding off, it's too smooth —
  add a fragment, vary the rhythm, or cut a clause.

### Tone calibration (five principles)

1. **Vary sentence length** — mix short with long; fragments are fine.
2. **Be concrete** — replace vague claims with an actual detail: a count, a
   prop, a specific line of dialogue.
3. **Let the mechanic carry the tone** — don't narrate the reader's
   reaction for them.
4. **Cut the neutrality** — a card can have a point of view (a warning, a
   tip, an aside) rather than reading like a manual.
5. **Earn the payoff** — don't tell the reader something is funny or
   dramatic; write the specific detail that makes it so.

### Never add these when rewriting

- A number, prop, or specific detail that wasn't in the original — invented
  specificity is worse than the vague version it replaces. Only add detail
  that's actually true of the game.
- Manufactured stakes ("this changes everything," "you'll never play the
  same way again").
- Forced first person or fake enthusiasm that wasn't in the source.

## Quick checklist before shipping a card

- [ ] No em dash followed by a simile
- [ ] No three-item adjective/reaction list closing a paragraph
- [ ] No "equal parts X and Y" or similar summarizing formula
- [ ] No word that tells the reader how to feel (hilarious, comedy gold, epic, unforgettable)
- [ ] No "the goal is to..." — instructions are stated directly
- [ ] At least one sentence under 10 words and one over 15 — rhythm is varied
- [ ] Not a run of clipped four-word sentences — that's the opposite failure
- [ ] Every instruction parses, and nothing is referred to before it's introduced
- [ ] Someone who has never seen this challenge could run it from the card alone
- [ ] It sounds like a person who wants you to play it, not a manual
- [ ] Read it aloud once — does it sound like a person, not a script?
